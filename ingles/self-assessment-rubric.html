Self-assessment rubric for the C1 oral interview (Cambridge C1 Speaking scales + ICAO Rating Scale)

What this document is for

The ENAIRE oral English test (evaluación oral del idioma inglés) is Phase 3, test A: "an interview aimed at measuring comprehension, expression, fluency and communicative skills" in English, guaranteeing at least level C1 of the CEFR (MCER). It can be face to face or online, is scored out of 50 points, 25 to pass, and is eliminatory. The results of tests B and C are only shown to those who pass it.

Verified facts (2025 bases and lists). The four aspects named in the bases (base 8, p. 12) are the only official criteria: comprehension (comprensión), expression (expresión), fluency (fluidez) and communicative skills (habilidades comunicativas). The mark counts in the final ranking (base 9, p. 14) and is the third tie-breaker. 2025 results (definitive list of 9 June 2026): 581 convened, 566 sat, 532 passed (94 %), 34 failed; marks were whole numbers from 25 to 50, average 40.1, median 41; 166 scored 45 or more and 33 scored 50. The spread among those who pass (25 points) is wider than that of the written test (10 points), so the oral moves the ranking more than its pass rate suggests. The interviews ran on the afternoon of the same day as tests B and C, one booking every 40 minutes. The parts, the length and the marking grid are not published; "about 30 minutes in five blocks" comes from academies.

ENAIRE doesn't publish its marking rubric. This file gives you two official proxies to apply to your own recordings:

  1. the Cambridge C1 Advanced Speaking assessment scales (the closest public description of what "C1 speaking" means), and
  2. the ICAO Language Proficiency Rating Scale (the aviation-English scale; level 4 "Operational" is the licence minimum, and ATC language is the world you'll work in).

Neither is ENAIRE's own rubric — don't translate your self-score into "ENAIRE points", the conversion is unknown. Use the scores as a trend line: the goal is to see the numbers rise week after week.

1. The Cambridge C1 Advanced Speaking scales

Format of the real Cambridge test, for context: 15 minutes in pairs, four parts, two examiners (one interlocutor who talks, one assessor who only listens and marks).

Part Time What you do
1 2 min Short conversation with the interlocutor: social and personal questions
2 1-minute "long turn" + 30s comment on your partner's turn (4 min total) Compare two of three photographs and speculate
3 4 min (2 min discussion + 1 min decision, ~15s to read the task) Collaborative task with your partner, written prompts
4 5 min Discussion of topics linked to Part 3

Two facts that matter: in Part 2 "candidates will never be asked to merely describe the visual" — they must speculate (hypothesise); and the handbook warns against closing with "That's all!" — keep talking until the examiner says "Thank you". Rehearsed speeches "are easily spotted and might be inappropriate for the questions asked" (Part 1).

The assessment scales for C1 (band 5 = best, band 1 = minimum acceptable, 0 = below band 1; bands 4 and 2 "share features of" the bands on each side). One row per criterion; quoted phrases are from the handbook.

Reading the scale correctly

  • The bands aren't marks out of 5 for "how good is my English" — band 3 is the target of a C1 candidate who passes; 5 is C2-like performance. The handbook doesn't state what raw score gives an overall pass, so treat "band 3 across the board" as your working target, not a guaranteed pass line.
  • Higher-level features to hunt for in your own speech (what separates a 5 from a 3): flexibility, "detailed" contributions, skilfully interweaving your contributions into the conversation.
  • Cambridge's own examiner commentaries on real candidates are useful evidence of what a marker writes down: quotations of the candidate's actual structures and words ("Having a clear representation…", "In the long term") and a list of their errors ("illude us", "utopistic", "the news are"). Copy this method: when you review a recording, write down 3 good structures and 3 errors with the exact words.

2. The ICAO Language Proficiency Rating Scale

  • Six criteria: pronunciation, structure, vocabulary, fluency, comprehension, interaction (pronunciación, estructura, vocabulario, fluidez, comprensión, interacción).
  • Six levels: 1 Pre-elementary, 2 Elementary, 3 Pre-operational, 4 Operational, 5 Extended, 6 Expert. Level 1 is defined only as "below Elementary".
  • The minimum for air traffic controllers and pilots is level 4. Level 6 never expires; levels 4 and 5 need periodic re-testing.
  • Your final ICAO level is the lowest of the six criteria — EUROCONTROL's ELPAC page states it explicitly: "the ICAO level awarded is determined by the lowest rating across the six criteria from both assessors; there is no compensatory marking." Apply the same rule to yourself: one weak criterion (typically pronunciation or comprehension under stress) sets your level.
  • ELPAC Paper 2 (speaking) is marked by two assessors — an English language expert and an operational expert (a controller or pilot) — with a third deciding if they disagree.

The five holistic descriptors (what a "proficient speaker shall" do): communicate effectively in voice-only and face-to-face situations; communicate on common, concrete and work-related topics with accuracy and clarity; use communicative strategies to exchange messages and to recognise and resolve misunderstandings (check, confirm, clarify); handle a complication or unexpected turn of events in a routine situation successfully and with relative ease; use a dialect or accent intelligible to the aeronautical community.

How C1 speaking relates to ICAO 4/5/6. There's no official table equating the two scales. A candidate who meets Cambridge C1 at band 3 in every criterion should comfortably be at or above ICAO level 4 in general language — but ICAO tests "common, concrete and work-related topics" and unexpected turns of events, while Cambridge tests abstract topics too. Treat them as complementary: Cambridge for the ENAIRE oral interview, ICAO for the aviation scenario blocks of the mock interviews and your future career.

On level 6: it requires a qualified rater's judgement, and "monolingual native speakers" are treated as probable expert speakers by default — for anyone else a formal procedure is needed. You don't need level 6 for the exam or the licence. Use level 5 as the ceiling of a good performance, not level 6.

3. How to score a recording: step by step

  1. Record every practice on your phone (audio is enough; video for Part 2 once a week).
  2. Don't score on the same day unless it's a mock; wait at least 3 hours — your first impression is too kind or too harsh.
  3. Pass 1, listening only (no transcript): score comprehension of your own speech — could a stranger follow it at first hearing? Mark where you'd have lost them.
  4. Pass 2, with a transcript (dictate it yourself, or type just the first 60 seconds): mark filler words, repeated phrases, false starts, wrong tense, wrong word, missing article.
  5. Score the five Cambridge criteria (0–5) from the evidence, not your mood. For each score, write one quotation of yours as evidence.
  6. Score the six ICAO criteria (2–6) only for the aviation block. Your ICAO level is the minimum of the six.
  7. Choose one target for the next recording, not five. One clear target per session produces more change than a long list.

7. Exam traps (for the oral)

  • Describing instead of speculating. Part 2 tasks always require speculation; describe in one sentence, then interpret, compare and evaluate.
  • Memorised answers. Rehearsed speeches are "easily spotted". Prepare structures and ideas, not scripts — the one exception is your 60–90 second opener about yourself, worth automating on its own.
  • Ending early ("That's all"). Keep going — add a consequence, a contrast, or a personal example.
  • Answers of one line. Also the opposite: monologues that take over. A personal-question answer is 30–60s.
  • Nervous acceleration. Speed hides errors from you, not from the examiner — pronunciation and stress placement suffer first.
  • Level inflation. Comparing yourself with other Spanish speakers rather than with the descriptor. Score against the words of the descriptor.
  • Confusing the ICAO minimum with the ENAIRE requirement. ICAO level 4 is the licence minimum after training; the ENAIRE interview is general English at C1. Passing one doesn't automatically prove the other.

8. Must memorize

  • Cambridge C1 criteria: Grammatical resource, Lexical resource, Discourse management, Pronunciation, Interactive communication; band 3 = C1 standard, band 5 = higher.
  • ICAO criteria: Pronunciation, Structure, Vocabulary, Fluency, Comprehension, Interaction; level 4 Operational, 5 Extended, 6 Expert; level = the lowest criterion.
  • ENAIRE oral: interview, C1, 50 points, pass at 25, eliminatory, face-to-face or online.
  • Cambridge Part 2: 1 minute, compare two pictures, speculate, never stop early.
  • Your habit: one recording, one target, one quotation of evidence.
