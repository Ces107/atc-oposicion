Listening resources and routines for the ENAIRE English path (oral interview, ESTEL, ICAO)

What the exam covers (where listening matters)

Where What is listened to Notes
ESTEL (English listening and comprehension test in FEAST I, Phase 2) An English listening and comprehension test. It's the one FEAST test you can actually prepare for, by listening to radio/TV in English, reading and talking to native speakers. FEAST does not test spoken English. There's a glossary of aviation words in the annex of the candidate leaflet. Source: EUROCONTROL FEAST information for candidates
Oral English interview (Phase 3 test A, C1) Understanding the examiner in real time, face to face or online; possibly an audio with gaps to fill and sentences to repeat after hearing them once. Comprehension is one of the four skills the 2025 bases measure; the audio-with-gaps and repeat-sentence blocks are an academy description, not ENAIRE's own
Written English test (Phase 1) Reading and use of English, C1, 20 questions. No listening.
Career later (ICAO / ELPAC) ELPAC Paper 1: 45-item computer-marked listening test in ATC context (four parts), based on radiotelephony recordings, routine and non-routine. Paper 2: live speaking. ELPAC leaflet says it takes around 40 minutes

Consequence: you need three kinds of listening: (1) general C1 English, mixed accents, natural speed (ESTEL and the interview); (2) fast, exact listening for numbers and instructions (aviation); (3) strategies to repair misunderstandings (comprehension and interaction). The rest of this file gives you resources and routines for each.

2. Accents: a listening map

The ICAO scale expects comprehension of "a range of speech varieties (dialect and/or accent) or registers" at level 5, and at level 4 it's "mostly accurate... when the accent or variety used is sufficiently intelligible for an international community of users." Cambridge C1 listening mixes British and other accents. Train the ear on many.

How to use it: one accent per week from week 3. Method: 3 minutes without transcript; write 3 keywords and 1 number you heard; listen again with the transcript; write the 5 words you misheard and why (linking, reduced vowel, unfamiliar name). Over the weeks, the misheard words tell you which sounds you don't process well.

Spanish-speaker traps to listen for: reduced vowels (schwa /ə/), weak forms (of, to, and, for), linking ("going to" as "gonna", "want to" as "wanna"), final consonant clusters, and the difference between ship/sheep and full/fool. Also the American flap /t/ in water, better and the British non-rhotic r (car, term).

3. Aviation audio

3.1 What's actually available

LiveATC doesn't cover Madrid-Barajas (LEMD); check LEBL (Barcelona) or LEVC (Valencia) yourself in a browser. Don't look for unofficial Spanish streams — there's no source saying they're legal, and whether recording/re-broadcasting ATC frequencies is legal in Spain isn't something to rely on either way. Use published, authorised recordings.

Mixed-accent practice without live audio: read the dialogues in the phraseology documents aloud using different voices; record yourself; play back at the end of the day.

3.2 What ATC English sounds like

  • "ICAO standardized phraseology shall be used in all situations for which it has been specified. Only when standardized phraseology cannot serve an intended transmission, plain language shall be used." (Annex 10 Vol II 5.1.1.1)
  • Standard words: Roger, Wilco, Affirm, Negative, Say again, Standby, Unable, Confirm, Correction, I say again — each with a fixed meaning (CAP 413 Table 8).
  • Communication error is the biggest causal factor in both level busts and runway incursions in Europe.
  • Numbers are said digit by digit: 0 ZERO, 1 WUN, 2 TOO, 3 TREE, 4 FOWER, 5 FIFE, 6 SIX, 7 SEVEN, 8 AIT, 9 NINER, decimal DAYSEEMAL, hundred HUN DRED, thousand TOUSAND; "FL100" is "flight level one hundred" (CAP 413 §2.13 and the numeral table).

4. The dictation routine

Active dictation — listen 30 seconds, transcribe, compare with the original — is the single best ESTEL-prep technique: it forces you to hear every word, you can't pretend to understand.

Materials: an audio player with a loop function, a text editor or paper, headphones. Best sources: the Cambridge tapescript (official, word-for-word), TED (subtitles close to literal), BBC 6 Minute English (approximate transcript: fine for gist/vocabulary, not for exact checking).

Steps (about 12 minutes per segment):

  1. Choose a 20–30 second segment (one or two speakers). Set a loop.
  2. Listen once without writing. Who, what, how many? (Gist.)
  3. Listen again and write what you hear, pausing after each sentence. Max 5 listens per sentence (5-minute timer — don't chase one word for 15 minutes).
  4. Mark every gap with "___". Don't use the transcript yet.
  5. Compare with the transcript. Three colours: red = missed, orange = heard wrongly (write what you wrote), green = correct.
  6. Classify each error in one word: vocabulary (didn't know it), sound (knew it, didn't recognise it), speed/linking, grammar (missed -s, -ed, auxiliary), attention (distracted).
  7. Repeat the segment aloud (shadowing, once), copying rhythm and stress.
  8. Log: words in segment, words wrong or missing, error type. Word error rate (WER) = errors ÷ words × 100.

Levels (progress only when you hit the target twice in a row):

Level Material Target
1 BBC Real Easy English, or the first two minutes of a 6 Minute English episode WER under 15%
2 6 Minute English, TED talks with clear speakers WER under 15% on 30s
3 Cambridge Part 2/3 audio; TED with accents (Indian, Irish, Australian) WER under 20%
4 Radio 4 speech, fast; non-native accents WER under 25%
5 (aviation) Number/callsign dictation (§6); ELPAC sample; short ATC phrases 0 errors in numbers

Variation for variety: alternate formats — gap-fill (transcript with 10 words removed), "detective" mode (find 3 words the presenter says that aren't in the transcript), translate the gist into Spanish in one sentence, predict the next sentence.

5. Note-taking for listening

You rarely need to write in the oral interview; in ESTEL and ELPAC you may hear numbers, names and sequences you have to hold for a few seconds. In ATC work, complex or lengthy taxi clearances should be noted down, and a correct read-back is the defence against errors.

System (one page):

  1. Layout: two columns. Left: keywords and numbers. Right: your own comments and questions.
  2. Abbreviations, invent once and keep: → (leads to), ↑ ↓ (increase, decrease), ≠ (different), = (same), b/c (because), w/ (with), w/o (without), # (number), ? (unclear), ! (important). Aviation: ↑ climb, ↓ descend, FL, HDG, QNH, R (right), L (left), RWY.
  3. Signposts to listen for: "however, on the other hand" (contrast); "first, second, finally" (list); "for example" (example); "in conclusion" (summary); "the main reason" (key point).
  4. Numbers: write digits, not words; write the unit next to them; check by repeating the digits aloud.
  5. After listening: cover the notes and say the summary aloud in 30 seconds, then compare.

Drill: listen to a 2-minute TED excerpt, note only 3 keywords and 2 numbers, summarise aloud. Repeat with 4-minute pieces. Target: a summary that names the main idea and two supporting points.

6. Number and callsign drills (aviation)

Callsigns, altimeter settings, flight levels, headings, wind speeds/directions, airspeed and frequencies are transmitted digit by digit: 3 = "TREE", 4 = "FOWER", 5 = "FIFE", 9 = "NINER", "FL100" = "flight level one hundred" (WUN HUN DRED). Frequencies: "122.1" is "one two two decimal one" (CAP 413 §2.13 and Table 4).

Drill A: numbers dictation (5 min).

  1. Generate 10 random sequences: a callsign (e.g. BIGJET 345), a heading (e.g. 140), a flight level (e.g. FL 240), a QNH (e.g. 1006), a frequency (e.g. 118.750). Dice or a random-number website.
  2. Say them aloud the aviation way, record.
  3. Next day, listen and write down what you hear without looking at the originals. Compare. Target: 0 errors.

Drill B: read-back pairs (7 min). Take the dialogues in the SKYbrary phraseology guide (e.g. "Big Jet 345, Metro Ground, taxi to holding point C, runway 27"). Read aloud in both roles; then a friend or tutor changes one item in the read-back (level, heading, runway) and you must detect it. Checking a read-back is easier if the information is given back in the same order — omissions are harder to catch than wrong data.

Drill C: "say again" strategy. For every sentence you don't understand, practise the correct reaction — "Say again", "Say again the last part", "Confirm heading" — instead of silence or guessing.

Exam traps

  • Trusting a transcript that isn't word-for-word. BBC 6 Minute English's transcript is approximate — use official tapescripts for exact dictation.
  • Listening only to native British/American speakers. The aeronautical community uses many accents.
  • Passive background listening. No feedback, no improvement.
  • Confusing "Roger" (message received) with "Wilco" (will comply) or "Affirm" (yes).
  • "Standby" means wait — it's not an approval or a denial, and isn't used for climb/descend instructions.
  • "Pan-pan" versus "mayday": urgency versus distress. "PAN PAN MEDICAL" in CAP 413 is a special term for protected medical transports.
  • Reading a read-back from memory instead of your note: write it down first.

Must memorize

  • ESTEL = the FEAST test you can improve through listening practice; FEAST doesn't test speaking.
  • ELPAC: Paper 1 listening (45 items, computer-marked), Paper 2 speaking (three tasks, two assessors); ICAO level = lowest of the six criteria.
  • Dictation loop: 30s → gist → write → compare → classify errors → shadow aloud → log WER.
  • Aviation numbers digit by digit: WUN, TOO, TREE, FOWER, FIFE, AIT, NINER; thousand = TOUSAND.
  • Standard words: roger, wilco, affirm, negative, say again, standby, unable, confirm, correction.
